The vendor who wanted to leave

Their case history showed why they wanted to leave. The same day, they chose to stay.

Situation

A high-volume vendor wanted to leave after 300+ cases in 30 days.

As a Selling Partner Support manager, a high-volume vendor contacted me because they wanted to leave.

Challenge

Under 50%

resolution across their 300+ cases in the previous 30 days

  • 40+ open cases when they contacted me, and 300+ cases in the previous 30 days.
  • Only about 10 of the 40+ open cases were new issues. All the others were follow-ups or unresolved problems, re-explained from zero every time.
  • By the vendor's own estimate, they had six-figure monthly sales blocked.

My impact

4

steps, only what I did myself

  1. I ran a dive deep on the case history, which showed the follow-up loop.
  2. I called the vendor directly.
  3. I explained the premium support option: a paid, dedicated service with direct channels and more freedom of action.
  4. I built the business case: a low fixed monthly fee, a tiny fraction of the sales at risk. The target was resolution from under 50% to at least 65-70%, roughly halving the losses, without giving up the rest of their sales on Amazon.

Result

Same day

the vendor subscribed to premium support and stayed

Seven-figure annual revenue vendor retained, still a premium support customer today

  • The vendor moved to a dedicated support tier with a much higher resolution rate.

Lesson

What I learned

Churn risk shows up in the case history long before it shows up in revenue.

What I'd do differently

I wouldn't wait for a vendor to threaten to leave: several open cases at once are already a signal. From that moment I made it a routine: every Monday I reviewed the team's cases by vendor and status (resolved, reopened, sent to tech support) to spot patterns early.

  • Business case writing

A global quality pilot, with a first-line view

The only first-line manager in a worldwide lead team: negative responses from above 65% to under 54% in 8 weeks.

Situation

Negative seller responses stuck above the goal, and supervisors feared more workload.

Reducing negative seller responses became the main focus of the business.

A worldwide pilot was announced at a roundtable and supervisors pushed back, worried about workload. I shared the operations point of view with the senior leader behind it and was invited into the pilot's lead team.

Challenge

Above 65%

negative response rate, against a goal of under 50%

  • Outcome accuracy was at 78.92% and the reopen rate at 14.25%.
  • There were no shared attributes to assess a seller interaction, and no structured way to decide who needed coaching first.
  • Supervisors doubted the goal was realistic and feared more workload.

My impact

5

steps, only what I did myself

  1. I was the only first-line manager in a senior worldwide lead team.
  2. I designed the assessment attributes and wrote the code of the audit form, with the development team supporting the implementation.
  3. With the quality managers, I defined a 3-tier audit mechanism to set coaching urgency, and made it possible to delegate audits to subject matter experts.
  4. I introduced the pilot in 4 training sessions and selected 51 supervisors, plus 51 in a mirror group. 2 office hours per week covered every time zone.
  5. I ran the 6-week pilot, with weekly completion checks and regular flash updates.

Result

8 weeks

to bring the negative response rate under 54%

After86.92%

Reopen rate

Before14.25%
After13.07%

Worldwide the assessment attributes I designed later became the global quality standard

  • Supervisors moved from pushback to asking to join future pilots, and the tiered audit mechanism was adopted beyond the pilot.

Lesson

What I learned

A project built on solid foundations brings benefits nobody planned. The goal was the negative response rate, but working on case quality also lifted outcome accuracy by 8 points.

What I'd do differently

I would build the learning path before launch. The Q&A office hours helped, but a short course and a support resource would have made the new method much easier for managers and teams.

Unblocking 100+ brand owners without waiting for the fix

A broken role assignment, proven with a test user and data: reopen rate from 100% to 7.3%, and 300+ cases resolved in 5 weeks.

Situation

Brand owners finished the required e-learning but never got the brand role.

In 2022 I managed the only team skilled in brand support at the time.

A global entertainment brand and other EMEA brand owners escalated: after completing the required e-learning, their representatives still did not get the brand role, so they could not manage listings, campaigns, permissions or brand images.

Challenge

0%

of these cases resolved: 100% of them reopened

  • Resolution on the whole queue was under 10%.
  • The procedure did not help, and the backlog was growing.

My impact

5

steps, only what I did myself

  1. I replicated the whole process with a test user: certificate issued, role still missing.
  2. I found the role assignment step was never triggered, and a manual grant needed Program Manager approval.
  3. I used queue data to prove an EMEA-wide failure and took it to the global Program Manager and the brand's vendor manager.
  4. The technical fix needed about 6 weeks, so I proposed a dedicated ticket route for manual role assignment. It was accepted.
  5. I upskilled 30 associates on the new route.

Result

300+

cases resolved in 5 weeks

Reopen rate on this issue

Before100%
After7.3%

Resolution on the whole queue

Beforeunder 10%
Afterabout 85%

100+ brand owners unblocked

  • What was left were the normal issues of any support queue, and the permanent technical fix followed.

Lesson

What I learned

Fix the root cause, prove it with data, involve the right people, and don't leave customers waiting six weeks for the permanent fix.

What I'd do differently

I would train my associates to raise their hand when similar cases pile up. One case is a ticket; ten similar cases are a pattern that needs a root cause view.

From a team of 30 to 10 promotions in a year

Ownership roles for a remote team of 30: job satisfaction from 3.5 to 4.7 and 10 promotions in one year.

Situation

My remote team grew to 30 people, and job satisfaction dropped.

In 2021 my team grew to 30 people, fully remote, as part of a pilot of larger teams.

Challenge

3.5 / 5

job satisfaction at the low point, with manager satisfaction at 4.2

  • With 30 people there is less one-to-one time, and a real risk that relationships become impersonal.

My impact

4

steps, only what I did myself

  1. I gave 16 associates 8 ownership roles matched to their career goals, so the team could run itself. Each role sent a monthly one-pager, and the roles were reshuffled every quarter.
  2. I was selected as owner of new hires and onboarding, and built the structure with my team: 130+ new hires onboarded and 100+ structured interviews run.
  3. The ownership-roles team supported the new hires during their ramp-up. My coordinator practised the supervisor role as their mentor, with a role guide I wrote. The role became the department's national standard.
  4. I opened weekly mock-interview slots, 2 hours a week for 6 months. All 10 people later promoted attended.

Result

10

team members promoted in one year

Job satisfaction

Before3.5/5
After4.7/5

Manager satisfaction

Before4.2/5
After5/5

91% probation success among the people I hired

  • My coordinator moved into her first leadership role, and her new-hire team ranked #1 in quality across the site for a full month.
  • Onboarding: +8% accuracy and -12% handling time.

Lesson

What I learned

A large team is both a risk and an opportunity. The more people you lead, the less one-to-one time you have. So people need to feel part of something and see their manager in the small things: catch-ups, feedback, delegation. Plus some healthy competition.

What I'd do differently

I wouldn't make every ownership role voluntary: training all the volunteers took a huge effort up front. I would choose one person per role, among those who wanted it, and pair them with someone eager to learn.

100+ new hires: a ramp-up tracked to day 90, adopted globally

Tracking new hires to day 90 instead of day 45: quality audit accuracy from 83.5% to 89%, now used for every new-hire batch worldwide.

Situation

The global new-hire deck stopped at 45 days and had no benchmarks.

In 2023-2024 I supervised all the new hires joining my department: 100+ people, across several skills and marketplaces.

The global deck that tracked them stopped at 45 days, mixed feedback from different sites and had no benchmarks.

Challenge

83.5%

new-hire quality audit accuracy

  • In my own team, the reopen rate was 12.4%, against an 8.5% target, and tenure was under 1 year.
  • The deck arrived just before the review meeting, with no time to analyze it.

My impact

3

steps, only what I did myself

  1. I got senior leadership approval to redesign the deck.
  2. With the trainers, I added a site view against a historical benchmark, extended tracking from 45 to 90 days, created a shared feedback template and moved the send to Monday, before the review.
  3. Inside my team I set up a dive deep group with 5 ownership roles: weekly case reviews, 3-tier coaching and an automatic email on controllable misses.

Result

100%

of new-hire batches worldwide tracked with the new deck

New-hire quality audit accuracy

Before83.5%
After89%

Reopen rate in my team, in 3 months

Before12.4%
After8.5%

1,500+ cases reviewed by my team's dive deep group

3 of 3 associates on a focus plan in my team improved and exited it

  • Handling time improved by about 8%.
  • The dive deep model was extended across the organization: 12 ownership roles and 1 coordinator.

Lesson

What I learned

Training doesn't end on the last day of class. The first 90 days decide how good a new hire becomes, so that is the window to measure and coach.

What I'd do differently

I would build the benchmark and the 90-day view before the first batch starts, instead of redesigning the deck once it was already running.

Trainers and local managers across Amazon's fulfillment network in Spain handled about 20 recurring tasks through different processes and data sources. Senior managers had no national view.

Challenge

3 to 7 hours

of admin per trainer, every week

  3. One access point: local managers see their site, senior managers see the whole country.

100%

of instructors nationally meeting their performance, quality and hours targets

Coaching data you can trust: from 1 in 4 errors to under 1%

A coaching classification tool in 11 languages: errors from about 25% to under 1%, now at 6 sites, with 83% adoption across the first 5.

Situation

About 1 in 4 coaching sessions ended up in the wrong category.

At my site, coaching sessions were logged as free text. Managers could not reliably tell which coaching was about quality and which about performance.

Challenge

~25%

of coaching sessions in the wrong category, about 1 in 4

  • Wrong categories made every coaching report unreliable.

My impact

3

steps, only what I did myself

  1. I built a coaching classification tool, with Claude Opus 5.0 as my coding partner.
  2. I ran a pilot at one site, then rolled it out to 6 sites in Spain.
  3. I made the interface work in 11 languages, with every status shown in color and in text, so colorblind users can read it.

Result

83%

adoption across the first 5 sites, 96% at the pilot site

Coaching sessions in the wrong category

Before~25%
Afterunder 1%

10,000+ coaching sessions classified in 13 weeks

Lesson

What I learned

Bad data usually starts with a hard form. When the right choice is the easy choice, the data fixes itself at the source.

What I'd do differently

I would have a backup working with me from day one, so the tool never depends on one person.

A readiness planner: from 10+ minutes to under 1 minute per decision

Spreadsheets replaced by one planner across 8 shifts, now the site's official planning resource.

Situation

Trainer planning and cross-training coverage lived in spreadsheets, across 8 shifts.

At my site, trainer planning and cross-training coverage lived in spreadsheets, across 8 shifts.

Challenge

10+ minutes

for each training coordination decision

  • No single view of how many cross-trained associates each process had against the expected volume.
  • Instructors and operations agreed every plan by hand.

My impact

3

steps, only what I did myself

  1. I built a training readiness planner that replaced the spreadsheets.
  2. It shows cross-trained associates against the expected volume, by process and shift.
  3. It includes an instructor planning app that writes the message for operations automatically.

Result

100%

of the expected volume covered by cross-trained associates, visible in one place

Time per training coordination decision

Before10+ minutes
Afterunder 1 minute
  • It became the site's official planning resource for L&D, area managers and flow leads, across 8 shifts.

Lesson

What I learned

A planning tool earns its place when it answers the question people ask at the start of the shift, on one screen.

What I'd do differently

I would bring area managers and flow leads into the first version, not only the trainers, so the tool fits operations from day one.
